The 10× moonshot

Can CLN5 and CLN6 families get to a shared natural history study 10 times faster?

In our illustrative model, a small family group spends about 3 years finding out what already exists, who to ask and whether another community's study fits, before anyone decides to launch a shared study. The Atlas already lists the registries, studies, groups and researchers for CLN5 and CLN6 disease, each with its source, so we think that groundwork could take about 3 to 4 months. The speed-up comes from discovery and preparation, not from biology, ethics review or regulation.

The milestone

Agree to launch a shared natural history study for CLN5 and CLN6 families

The milestone is reached when the partners agree to launch: who takes part, which existing registry or study it builds on, and what data it collects. Ethics review, contracts and enrollment come after and are not part of the 10× claim.

Before a therapy can be tested, researchers need to know how a disease unfolds without treatment. The two disease-specific natural history studies the Atlas lists have each followed one disease on its own: 37 people in the Neurogene CLN5 study and 30 in the Nationwide Children's CLN6 study, as listed on ClinicalTrials.gov. A shared study built on registries that already exist could give future trials a clearer baseline and give families a way to take part now.

      Where the time goes

      The assumptions behind 10×

      1. Most of today's 3 years goes into finding and judging, not into science.

        Why we think so. This is our working estimate, not a measurement. Our plan assumes related communities and assets are found by word of mouth and cold outreach, often over months to more than a year. The challenge brief describes groups rebuilding assets they cannot find, and says that judging diseases one at a time takes a scout months per mechanism.

        How to test it. Interview patient organization leaders who have set up a study and time each phase they went through.

      2. The building blocks already exist and can be found in public sources.

        Why we think so. From public records alone the Atlas found a recruiting registry that lists both CLN5 and CLN6, a natural history study for each disease, a family register and sheep models for both, each with a source you can open. Coverage still depends on what public sources hold.

        How to test it. Ask NCL clinicians and patient groups what we missed, and measure how much of their list the Atlas already holds.

      3. Building on an existing registry is faster than designing a new study.

        Why we think so. DEM-CHILD is one registered natural history study for all NCL forms, including CLN5 and CLN6, run from the Hamburg NCL clinic, and a published paper has already used its records of 15 children with CLN5. Data fields and outcome measures may not need to be invented again.

        How to test it. Ask the DEM-CHILD team whether a shared CLN5 and CLN6 effort fits their current consent and data access, and how long joining would take.

      4. Similar symptoms and course are enough for a shared observational study, even though the mechanisms differ.

        Why we think so. Both diseases build up subunit c and share informative symptoms. A natural history study measures how symptoms change over time, not whether a drug works, so we expect the mechanism difference to matter less here than it would for a shared trial.

        How to test it. Have NCL clinicians review whether the same outcome measures and visit schedule work for both diseases.

      5. Expert time can be focused on a short checklist instead of open-ended reading.

        Why we think so. Every link in the Atlas carries a status and a source, and the Atlas lists the questions that need review, so an expert checks a handful of points rather than starting from a blank page.

        How to test it. Time an expert reviewing the Atlas checklist against the same review done from scratch, and record where they disagree with the Atlas.

      6. A sourced proposal gets answered sooner than a cold email.

        Why we think so. A message that names the shared registry, the shared biology and its limits gives the reader a concrete reason to reply. This is untested.

        How to test it. Run a pilot with one real patient organization and track reply rates and time to a first meeting.

      7. The speed-up ends at the decision to launch.

        Why we think so. Ethics review, contracts, enrolment and the biology itself do not get faster because information is easier to find, so we leave them out of the 10× claim.

        How to test it. Track the time after the decision separately in any pilot, so the claim cannot quietly grow.

        Built with OpenAI

        AI does the reading. Sources do the deciding.

        OpenAI models help in three narrow places. Everything else in the Atlas follows rules you can inspect, and every claim keeps the source it came from.

        1. 1

          Extract

          Reads research abstracts and pulls out what is linked to what, with the exact sentence as proof. A claim is dropped when its quote cannot be found word for word in the source.

        2. 2

          Reconcile

          The same disease, gene or group is often written differently from one source to the next. When names do not match exactly, the model picks one of at most five known records, or none. It never makes up a new one.

        3. 3

          Explain

          Turns a chain of cited links into plain sentences for families, or precise ones for researchers. Every sentence cites one link and keeps its status, and what is not known is listed too.

        Try it: open a connection in the Atlas, or a route in the journey, and choose Explain in plain language. Answers are labelled Written by OpenAI from the linked sources. If the writer is not available, the guide explains instead, and the cited links are always shown.

        Trust and privacy

        Know where everything comes from.

        Every study, link and note on One Mission carries a label that says who it comes from. What you write about yourself stays in this browser, and you can delete it here at any time.

        What the labels mean

        What is sent, and what is not

        • Sent: your searches and the pages you open go to the Atlas server so it can answer. There is no account, so nothing is tied to your name.
        • Sent, as totals only: for studies published by research teams, One Mission counts views, screenings started and completed, potential matches and contact clicks. The team sees the numbers, never your answers or who you are.
        • Never sent: your screening answers, your age and country, your plan and your timeline. They stay in this browser.
        • Your messages to research teams go from your own email or the team's contact page. One Mission does not see or keep them.
        • The guide is an AI assistant run by ElevenLabs. What you type or say to it is sent there. Do not share health details with it that you want to keep private.
